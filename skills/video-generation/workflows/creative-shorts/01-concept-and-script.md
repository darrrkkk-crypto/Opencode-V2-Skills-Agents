# 01 · Concept and script

Use to develop an idea into a short or revise an established story. The script answers who is where, what they do, and what they say; leave shot numbers, shot sizes, camera moves, and seconds to shot design

## Start from a core that works on its own

Develop characters and causality around an image or concept that can stand on its own. A short has a different capacity from a feature or series, so its core needs enough focus

Start from an object with a story, an extreme constraint, or a key choice. Examples include an unopened letter, a story taking place entirely inside a moving car, or someone outside a door deciding whether to knock or leave

The three starting points can be made more concrete:

- An object with a story: a folded paper boat, a letter left unopened for a long time, a desk lamp that stays on late into the night
- An extreme constraint: the story happens only inside a moving car, only while it rains, or with a protagonist who remains silent throughout
- A key choice: knock or leave; return a found object or quietly put it in a bag

If removing the central image leaves the story completely intact, the image is only decoration. First make the one-sentence logline explain the character, desire or disturbance, visible action, and result, then develop the body. Once people, places, and objects are named, keep those names consistent

A short can use the structure “something is already happening → a disturbance enters → the character acts and leaves a result.” Focus on one theme, usually one or two characters and one or two locations, following an event already underway; the ending can be open while providing an independent, complete viewing experience

```text
Situation (something is already happening)
  → Disturbance (a concrete event or outside force enters)
    → Action or result (the lead reacts, and a aftertaste remains)
```

A very short film can use just one reversal: the first half establishes an expectation, and the second breaks it. Keep names of people, places, and objects once locked, because later reference images, shot designs, and model slots depend on them

When a contemporary feel is needed, use the [reference method in preflight](00-preflight.md#when-to-reference-contemporary-work) to find structural and visual inspiration; otherwise, develop the work around its own concept

## Something must already be happening in the opening

Check the opening at 2, 5, and 10 seconds: first see or hear a concrete event, then recognize who is doing what, then encounter a question. Social videos especially need information, movement, and sound to engage viewers together in the first two seconds. Estimate seconds during the check, while keeping the script body to scenes, action, and dialogue

Bring the central image forward where possible: lights going out one by one, a person wet on only one shoulder, a hand paused on a switch. Sound can also catch attention; a door bolt or “Don't open the door” can precede explanation. If deleting the first section leaves the film unaffected, it is usually still setup

### Check the opening at 2, 5, and 10 seconds

| Checkpoint | What viewers should receive | Concrete image or sound |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First 2 seconds | An engaging event is immediately clear, with movement and sound entering at once | A door bursts open with “Don't come in,” or a letter slides quickly across a counter and stops beside a hand as the scraping sound abruptly ends |
| First 5 seconds | Understand that someone is doing something | Who, where, and what is in their hand |
| First 10 seconds | A disturbance or question has appeared | Someone is outside, a letter is unopened, a hand pauses on a switch, making viewers wonder what happens next |

Social viewers can swipe away, so the opening must make them want the next second. A longer film may have room to develop later, but its opening still needs to hold viewers; for works lasting a dozen or a few dozen seconds, the opening already takes up a large proportion and needs to enter the event even more directly. A creative short primarily brings its central image forward, rather than opening with an advertising selling point or a next-episode cliffhanger

### Catch attention with an object, sound, or line

A paper boat, unopened letter, or late-night desk lamp should appear at the very start, or the opening should show the instant just before it appears. Sound can catch the ear first: a switch click, a glass door opening, a cooler suddenly falling quiet, or a line whose explanation is delayed. Sound can still create an event while the image is quiet

For a dialogue-led opening, make the line short, concrete, and forceful. “Don't open the door” moves the situation more than explaining that someone does not want to work overtime; the force of delivery and the room sound left after the line are both part of the opening. Carry that force into the later timbre lock

Possible opening techniques:

- Something changing: lights go out section by section, rain wets only one side of the street
- An unusual detail: a person wet on only one shoulder, with no shopping basket in hand
- An unfinished action: a hand paused on a switch
- Production sound preceding the image: a door bolt, brakes, a match striking
- One forceful line: short and specific, leaving suspense after it is said

Try reading with the first section deleted: if the work is still the same, keep turning walking, weather, or backstory setup into an event that actually happens

## Write a filmable script body

Keep the structure to scene headings, action, character names, and dialogue. Scene headings specify interior or exterior, location, and day or night; express character emotions through bodies, objects, and visible states

A narrative short has at least one exchange that changes the situation, reveals a relationship, or forces a choice; keep dialogue concise while retaining effective conflict. Voice-over only adds background that cannot be said naturally. Follow the user's specified direction for explicit no-dialogue or narration-led requests

Write rain, a switch click, cooler hum, hurried breathing, a keyboard stopping, and a door sound as concrete events in the scene, designed together with dialogue and action. If a brand needs to appear, make it an object or device that moves the plot

Pure factual exposition can become questions and answers, doubts, confirmations, or commands between characters, letting a key line change the next action, reveal information, or force a choice. State interior or exterior, the specific location, and day or night in scene headings, with each scene doing one thing where possible. Leave shot sizes, camera moves, shot numbers, and timestamps to the shot-design document; retain events viewers can see and hear in the script

### Complete script example

```text
# Concept and Script

One-sentence logline: After closing, the clerk is about to turn off the lights when she finds someone outside who is wet on only one shoulder.
Central image: In the cool white glow of the cooler, her hand is still on the switch, and a blurred silhouette shows through the glass door.

——

Scene 1  INT.  CONVENIENCE STORE  NIGHT

Rain keeps hitting the glass door. Most of the store is already dark; only the cooler is still lit.
The clerk hits the switch for the last row of bento cases. Warm lights go out one by one.

CLERK
Alright.

She hears the door being pushed open. Someone wet on only one shoulder walks in, no shopping basket in hand.
The two of them look at each other across the counter. The cooler hums low.

The visitor sets an unopened letter on the counter. The clerk does not reach for it.

Scene 2  EXT.  CONVENIENCE STORE ENTRANCE  NIGHT

The clerk stands in the doorway. The letter is still in her hand.
The visitor is already walking away. The rain seems to wet only one side of the street.
```

## Start writing from the user's materials

When an idea, image, reference video, or product description provides enough support, form the script first and fill gaps in the materials gradually in the draft. The template below produces only a story document; the task scope determines whether shot design or generation follows

### Complete writing template

```text
Your task now is to write a creative-short script that can be watched in one sitting. It is not a series, and it is not a shot list.

Known information:
- Form: {genre}
- Rough duration: {seconds} (this only helps you judge story capacity; do not write seconds into the script)
- Aspect ratio: {aspect ratio}
- User’s original request: {original request}
- Already confirmed preflight conditions: {briefly summarize if any; write “none” if none}
- Needs internet feel: {if yes, benchmark references first; if no, do not force trendy elements}

Write in the following structure:
If internet feel is needed: first summarize the benchmark reference in one sentence (borrow structure and visual highlights; do not copy plot), then write the body.
If internet feel is not needed: stay pure. Do not bolt on cyberpunk, holograms, or wasteland just to chase a hit.

1. One-sentence logline: a [kind of person], because of [a concrete desire or outside disturbance], does [a visible action], and ends with [a visible result or aftertaste]. If that is hard to compress into one sentence, first offer two more focused directions.
2. Central image: describe one core picture. Who, in what kind of space, holding what.
3. Opening: the thing that happens immediately in the first scene. It can be an object, an off action, a bright production sound, or an impactful line. Ideally it is the central image itself, or the instant just before the central image appears. Do not start from waking up, walking, or introducing the weather.
4. What we will not write: list elements this story should deliberately avoid.

Next, write the script body. Keep only scene headings, action, character names, and dialogue.

Writing constraints:
- No more than {N} speaking characters, no more than {M} locations.
- The story starts from “something already happening.”
- Unless no dialogue is explicitly specified, design at least one character exchange that moves the situation; voice-over is only a light supplement and must not lead the plot.
- Write production sound that syncs with action into the script, including lip-synced lines, ambient sound, and key action sounds.
- No complex side plots, no flashbacks to explain origin stories, and the ending cannot be “to be continued.”
- The script must never contain shot size, camera move, shot numbers, or timestamps.
- If there is a brand element, it can only be an object in the scene or a device that moves the plot.
- Names of people, places, and objects must stay consistent. Do not replace them with synonyms later.
- If the story feels too crowded, cut hard until only one thing remains.
```

## Delivery and self-check

First deliver an editable script document: a one-sentence logline, central image, body, and explicitly excluded elements. Confirm that the opening works, the core conflict survives removing voice-over, every scene has a function, the ending is visible, and names stay consistent

Check each item:

- Can one sentence explain the story, and does an opening image, sound, or line catch attention first?
- Do dialogue and action move the situation, and does the core conflict still work without supplementary voice-over?
- Are dialogue, ambient sound, and action sounds designed as synced production events?
- Can you picture the ending with your eyes closed, and would deleting any scene harm the story's completeness?
- Review technical terms such as medium shot, close-up, push in, and 0s, and move shot-execution information into shot design
- Are the names of people, places, and objects identical from start to finish?

Record the one-sentence logline, central image, script body, and content boundaries in that order. If there is a lot of content, split it into readable documents for the user to edit directly. A script-only revision can end after delivering the script document
